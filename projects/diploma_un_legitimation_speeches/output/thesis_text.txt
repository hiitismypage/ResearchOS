Abstract 
How states publicly justify legally contested conduct before a universal institutional audience has 
received theoretical attention but limited empirical study. This thesis examines legitimation strategies 
employed by state leaders in UN General Assembly General Debate speeches, drawing on a 
criterion-based sample of 33 case-years (102 manually coded segments) Harvard UN General Debate 
Corpus. Directed qualitative content analysis, structured around Habermas’s typology of social action, is 
applied alongside an eight-category manual coding framework. Counter-terrorism and securitization 
framing (34.3%) and procedural-legal claims (27.5%) are the dominant strategy types; strategic action is 
absent as a dominant mode across the sample. A significant temporal shift marks the post-2001 rise of 
communicative-justificatory framing, partially reversed after 2014. Cross-bloc convergence in 
counter-terrorism rhetoric coexists with divergence in legitimation register: Western states rely 
predominantly on justificatory framing while non-Western states invoke formal Charter provisions more 
consistently. The findings extend corpus-based evidence for deliberative legitimacy and document 
structurally conditioned asymmetries in how geopolitical blocs relate to the postwar normative 
architecture. 
Keywords: legitimation strategies, UN General Assembly, communicative action, Habermas, 
international law, norm contestation, diplomatic discourse, geopolitical blocs

Introduction 
When a state uses military force in circumstances that other governments characterize as illegal, 
the diplomatic calendar offers a predictable ritual. At the United Nations General Assembly, its head of 
state or foreign minister takes the floor and constructs a public justification. The justification may invoke 
self-defense under Article 51 of the UN Charter, reframe the operation as counter-terrorism, appeal to 
humanitarian necessity, contest the credibility of the accusing parties, or some combination of these 
moves. What varies is the specific configuration and the rhetorical register. What does not vary – and this 
is the central empirical observation motivating this thesis – is that the move is made – publicly, on the 
record, in the one forum where virtually every state in the international system holds a seat – a body of 
193 member governments. 
This practice appears across a wide range of states, regardless of their material capabilities, 
political systems, or ideological positions. This raises an important question for international relations 
theory: why do states invest diplomatic resources in publicly justifying the use of force, even when the 
decision to act has already been made? States do not usually rely on military power alone. They also seek 
to present their actions as lawful, necessary, defensive, humanitarian, or consistent with broader principles 
of international order. This suggests that legitimacy matters in international politics. It is not simply a form 
of propaganda or a diplomatic afterthought. 
The scholarly literature on legitimation in international relations has grown substantially over the 
past three decades. Theoretical accounts now range from Franck’s (1990) early work on the compliance 
pull of legitimate rules, through Hurd’s (1999) social constructivist account of legitimacy as belief, to 
Bjola’s (2005) application of Habermasian communicative action theory to the justification of force, and 
Wiener’s (2014) framework for norm contestation across institutional settings. What this body of work 
does not provide is a systematic, corpus-based account of which legitimation strategies actually appear in 
leaders’ speeches at the UNGA, in what combinations, and how the repertoire has changed from the 
founding of the United Nations to the present. The existing empirical literature is built predominantly 
from case studies of individual episodes – NATO’s intervention in Kosovo in 1999 (Bjola, 2005; Head, 
2008), the US-led invasion of Iraq in 2003 (Rapp, 2022), the Suez crisis of 1956 (Rapp, 2022) – each of 
which yields analytic depth but cannot speak to aggregate patterns, frequency distributions, or longitudinal 
evolution. This thesis addresses that gap. 
Three research questions structure the analysis. The main question asks: What legitimation 
strategies are employed by state leaders in speeches delivered at the UN General Assembly General 
Debate to justify conduct alleged to violate international law, from 1946 to 2023? Three sub-questions

The study proceeds from four theory-driven hypotheses. H1 addresses rhetorical convergence: 
after September 2001, the adoption of counter-terrorism framing increased across all geopolitical blocs 
without statistically significant bloc-based differentiation – a pattern consistent with ideational 
convergence rather than bloc-specific rhetorical divergence. This hypothesis is falsifiable: had one bloc 
demonstrated systematically greater uptake of counter-terrorism language than others, or had pre-9/11 
inter-bloc differences persisted into the post-2001 period, H1 would be rejected. H2 addresses the 
character of the legitimation repertoire: the dominant strategies deployed across the corpus – self-defense 
and necessity claims, counter-terrorism framings, sovereignty and non-interference appeals, and 
procedural-legal invocations of the UN Charter – function predominantly as normative-legal action in 
Habermas’s typology, oriented toward shared legal frameworks rather than toward purely strategic evasion

The empirical basis of the analysis is the Harvard UN General Debate Corpus (UNGDC), a 
machine-readable dataset of all speeches delivered at the General Assembly’s annual General Debate from 
1946 to 2023 – 11,127 speeches from 198 countries. The analytical sample is identified through a 
criterion-based procedure: state-years in which the state-sessions in which a government faced recorded 
accusations of conduct whose legality or normative status was disputed are selected on the basis of United 
Nations documentation – resolutions and meeting records of the General Assembly, Security Council 
proceedings, special rapporteur reports), NGO documentation (Amnesty International, Human Rights 
Watch), and academic secondary literature on specific conflicts and episodes. Python scripts assist in 
extracting and keyword-filtering the corresponding speeches from the full corpus; every identified speech

is then read and coded manually. The coding employs directed qualitative content analysis (Hsieh & 
Shannon, 2005) and proceeds in two stages. In the first stage, descriptive mapping establishes the 
frequency and co-occurrence of coded strategies across the full sample. In the second stage, comparative 
qualitative interpretation examines how specific strategies are realized across actors, periods, and 
normative contexts. The coding framework operates along two analytical dimensions: the type of alleged 
violation addressed, and the legitimation strategy deployed in response. Both dimensions are derived 
deductively from existing theory and refined through iterative engagement with the corpus material. The 
qualitative findings are supplemented by descriptive frequency counts and co-occurrence mapping across 
the full period 1946–2023. 
Several limitations require stating at the outset. The corpus covers only the UNGA General 
Debate; positions articulated in closed Security Council sessions, bilateral correspondence, or domestic 
political discourse fall outside the scope of analysis. The speeches examined represent a specific genre – 
formal, high-stakes, public address before a global institutional audience – and the legitimation strategies 
identified here should not be assumed to correspond to the full range of legal or political argumentation 
available to states in other settings. No claim about the actual legality of the conduct in question is made 
anywhere in this thesis: the designation “alleged violation” in every case signals an independently 
documented accusation, not a legal finding. The analysis examines how states respond to that accusation 
in public discourse, not whether the accusation was legally correct. A further constraint concerns 
non-English-language speeches: while the UNGDC provides translated versions, strategic ambiguity or 
omissions in translation may affect the precision of coding for certain passages. 
The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 1 reviews the relevant literature across three 
overlapping fields: theories of legitimacy and legitimation in international relations, the UNGA General 
Debate as a rhetorical and institutional arena, and existing empirical research on legitimation discourse in 
multilateral settings. Chapter 2 develops the theoretical framework, moving from Habermas’s (1984) 
typology of social action and validity claims through its IR applications in Risse (2000), Müller (2004), 
and Bjola (2005), to an operational account of legitimation strategy for the purposes of this study. Chapter 
3 describes the research design, corpus selection, sampling logic, and coding framework in detail. Chapter 
4 presents the empirical findings: frequency and co-occurrence patterns, comparative case analysis across 
three dominant strategy types, and a longitudinal account of how the repertoire has shifted across the 
period 1946–2023. Chapter 5 interprets these findings against the theoretical framework, assesses all four 
hypotheses, and identifies the study’s contributions and limitations. The conclusion returns directly to the 
main research question and states what the analysis has established.

1. Literature Review 
1.1. Legitimacy in International Relations: Theoretical Landscape 
Legitimacy has become an increasingly important, if contested, concept in IR scholarship on the 
postwar international order. Its theoretical status shifted substantially over the past three decades, moving 
from a largely normative concern – whether particular norms or institutions deserved compliance – toward 
a more empirical and relational question of how legitimacy functions as a social fact structuring state 
behavior. 
Thomas Franck’s (1990) foundational contribution established the link between legitimacy and 
rule compliance within the international legal system. Franck’s central question – why states comply with 
international legal rules in the absence of centralized coercive enforcement – led him to the concept of 
compliance pull: the force that legitimate rules exert on state behavior independently of coercive 
sanctions. He identified four properties that assess how strongly a given rule commands compliance: 
whether its formulation is clear and precise enough to guide behavior, whether recognized procedural 
markers confer authenticity upon it, whether it sits consistently within the surrounding body of legal 
principle, and whether it occupies a defined position within an established normative hierarchy. The 
framework treated legitimacy as an observable quality of rules rather than a purely philosophical attribute, 
and positioned it as a matter of degree rather than a binary judgment. This insight provides a starting point 
for analysing why states seek to attach contested conduct to legal norms that retain recognized legitimacy. 
Hurd (1999) offered an equally important reorientation. Departing from coercion- and 
interest-based accounts of norm compliance, Hurd argued that legitimacy operates as a third mechanism of 
social control – alongside coercion and self-interest – grounded in internalized belief in the rightness of 
norms. As Hurd defines it, legitimacy is “the normative belief by an actor that a rule or institution ought to 
be obeyed” – a subjective, relational quality constituted by the actor’s perception of the norm rather than 
by the norm’s objective properties (Hurd, 1999, p. 381). The critical move was to treat legitimacy as a 
social fact rather than an evaluative standard: what matters analytically is not whether a norm meets an 
external criterion of justice but whether relevant actors believe it to be binding. This constructivist 
reframing opened legitimacy to empirical analysis: if it is constituted through belief and practice, it can be 
traced in the public statements and justificatory moves of state actors – precisely the objects this study 
examines. 
Gronau and Schmidtke (2016) positioned legitimation as an active, top-down practice rather than a 
passive outcome of norm compliance. Building on Weberian ideas about legitimacy and domination, they 
developed a typology of legitimation strategies organized around three distinct constituencies: member 
 

--- PAGE 10 ---
10 
states, institutional staff, and wider publics. While their empirical focus was on international institutions – 
the G8 and the IMF during periods of contested authority – their conceptual architecture can be adapted to 
state actors addressing multilateral audiences. 
Wiener’s (2014) norm contestation framework provides a further theoretical resource. Where 
earlier constructivist accounts treated legitimacy as a relatively stable attribute of particular rules 
reproduced within established communities of practice, Wiener argues that contestation is itself a 
norm-generative social practice: through acts of challenging, questioning, and justifying, actors 
continuously re-enact the normative structure of meaning-in-use, which is the basis of legitimacy in global 
governance (Wiener, 2014). Legitimation, in this view, is not a one-off act of compliance or defection but 
a running negotiation over what a norm means and who it binds. This has direct implications for the 
temporal scope of the present study: the evolution of legitimation strategies from 1946 to 2023 reflects an 
ongoing contestation over the terms of the postwar normative architecture, not a fixed repertoire deployed 
against a fixed standard. 
1.2. Diplomacy as Public Communication: the UNGA as a Rhetorical Arena 
The United Nations General Assembly General Debate is one of the most regularized and visible 
genres of public diplomatic communication in the international system. Convened annually each 
September, it provides every member state with a designated speaking slot before the full plenary – an 
opportunity that governments consistently use to state positions on contested norms, respond to 
international criticism, and present their preferred interpretation of international events before a universal 
audience. The ritual character of the event – fixed institutional setting, established speaking order, 
universal participation, near-identical format from year to year – makes it unusually tractable for 
systematic analysis. Genre constraints are relatively uniform across decades and states, which reduces the 
interpretive noise that arises when comparing speech events from radically different institutional contexts. 
The Harvard UN General Debate Corpus (UNGDC), introduced by Baturo, Dasandi, and 
Mikhaylov (2017), made systematic text-as-data analysis of the General Debate feasible for the first time. 
The original dataset comprises 7,314 statements from 198 countries covering sessions 1970–2014; the 
corpus has since been extended on Harvard Dataverse to cover sessions from 1946 to 2023, comprising 
11,127 speeches in its current release. Baturo et al. (2017) demonstrate that GD speeches can serve as 
empirical proxies for state preferences precisely because they are less constrained than UNGA roll-call 
votes: unlike voting, where aid relationships and strategic blocs shape behaviour, states deliver their 
addresses without direct institutional stakes, which means the choice of topics and emphases more freely 
reflects national priorities. Representatives of the Finnish and German missions to the UN, interviewed by 
 

--- PAGE 11 ---
11 
the authors, described GD speeches as the place where states “speak their mind” and “the most sovereign 
thing that a country does as a member of the UN” (Baturo et al., 2017, p. 2). Preference scores derived 
from the corpus have been cross-validated against independent foreign policy indicators, establishing their 
construct validity across multiple measurement approaches. Research drawing on this dataset has 
concentrated primarily on recovering ideological alignment and positional signals; the analytical 
framework here treats the same textual material with a qualitatively oriented analytical framework applied 
to a theoretically motivated sub-sample. A relevant limitation is that GD speeches are strategically 
constructed performances: as Baturo et al. (2017, p. 3) note, “member states present themselves 
exclusively in the guise in which they wish to be known.” For the purposes of the present analysis, 
however, this is a feature rather than a flaw – the strategic construction of public justification is precisely 
the object of inquiry. 
The General Debate occupies an ambiguous position between communicative and strategic action 
– a distinction central to the theoretical framework developed in Chapter 2. On one account, it functions as 
a site of public deliberation: governments advance positions, respond to challenges, and expose their 
reasoning to scrutiny before a universal and formally equal audience. On another, it operates as a 
performance arena where states manage reputational risks, address domestic audiences through 
international ones, and deploy available normative vocabulary without genuine openness to argumentative 
revision. Neither account fully captures the phenomenon. What can be said is that the General Debate is 
the most consistently attended, most formally institutionalized, and most universally accessible farena 
where heads of state and foreign ministers place their governments' legal and normative positions on the 
record before a universal audience — a feature that makes it the analytically appropriate venue for 
studying legitimation strategies across the full membership of the United Nations rather than one episode 
at a time. 
1.3. Existing Research on Legitimation Discourse in UN Speeches: Contributions and Gaps 
The scholarly literature on the legitimation of contested conduct in multilateral settings has 
generated a body of analytically rich case studies that establish the importance of the phenomenon while 
leaving systematic questions unanswered. 
Bjola (2005) provides the most theoretically developed account within this tradition. Applying 
Habermas’s theory of communicative action to the NATO military campaign in Kosovo (1999) and the 
coalition-led military action in Iraq (2003), Bjola contends that the degree of legitimacy accorded to 
military action cannot be settled by reference to UN Charter compliance alone. His concept of deliberative 
legitimacy, defined as “the non-coerced commitment of an actor to obey a norm adopted on the basis of 
 

--- PAGE 12 ---
12 
the criteria and rules reached through a process of communicative action” (Bjola, 2005, p. 266), 
introduces a procedural standard that distinguishes genuine normative justification from strategic 
persuasion. The diagnostic question is whether the promoters of intervention genuinely sought “a reasoned 
consensus on the need for the use of force” or merely engaged in “power games based on credible threats, 
intimidation or rhetorical exchanges with no visible intention to achieve argumentative consensus” (Bjola, 
2005, p. 267). Assessed against this standard, the two cases yield contrasting findings. In the Kosovo 
episode, alliance members modified their positions in response to arguments and the process, however 
imperfect, remained genuinely open to deliberative revision. The Iraq episode was dominated by strategic 
action: the United States manipulated the legal framing of Resolution 1441 without openness to 
argumentative revision, seeking agreement rather than understanding. The analytical value of this 
distinction is considerable; its empirical scope is not. Two cases, both Western-led, selected for their 
visibility rather than their representativeness, cannot speak to whether similar patterns obtain across the 
full range of legitimation contexts the postwar system has generated. 
Head (2008) extends the Habermasian approach into a more explicitly critical register, 
demonstrating that patterns of “exclusion, coercion and illegitimacy… not only challenge the claims to 
legitimacy of the intervention and of the interveners, but demonstrate the critical power of a 
communicative framework” (Head, 2008, p. 150). The Kosovo episode supplies the evidence: Kosovar 
Albanians were structurally absent from Security Council deliberations – having no state and therefore no 
seat – which meant the principal affected party had no standing in a process nominally conducted on its 
behalf. The Rambouillet negotiations reproduced the same asymmetry, with the Contact Group setting 
terms while the threat of air strikes functioned as an instrument of pressure rather than persuasion. Head’s 
concern is normative, but the analytical observation generalizes: case-study methods can identify such 
structural dynamics with precision, but cannot measure their prevalence, frequency, or variation across the 
diversity of the UN membership and the full postwar period. 
Rapp (2022) approaches the problem through an entirely different method. Drawing on 
declassified archival documents from British and American decision-makers, he traces the internal 
reasoning behind legal justifications for force in the Suez crisis (1956) and the Grenada military 
intervention (1983). His central claim is that “in a deeply legalized system actors feel compelled to portray 
their behaviors as legal, even if these justifications are tenuous or if pursuing them is time and resource 
consuming” (Rapp, 2022, p. 337). The justificatory preference followed a clear hierarchy: references to 
codified treaty obligations were preferred over customary international law, and both were preferred over 
non-legal rationales, because written legal text offers a standard that opponents find harder to dismiss. In 
 

--- PAGE 13 ---
13 
the Suez case, British officials sought not the legally strongest argument but the most palatably legal one 
– the formulation most capable of managing anticipated criticism. In the Grenada case, the United States 
assembled three individually weak grounds into a composite justification – an invitation from the 
Governor-General, a threat to American citizens, and authorization under the OECS regional treaty – 
because no single argument was persuasive in isolation. The contribution is significant, establishing that 
legitimation is a strategic and resource-intensive practice even for preponderant states. The method, 
however, forecloses analysis of the public dimension: the speeches delivered to the UN General Assembly 
in these same episodes remain unexamined, and the gap between internal decision-making and public 
justification is itself an analytically interesting object. 
Taken together, this body of work establishes several important premises: that the legitimation of 
legally contested conduct is a systematic practice across a wide range of states and contexts; that the UN 
General Assembly and the Security Council are the primary arenas for its public expression; and that the 
gap between formal legal compliance and perceived legitimacy is analytically tractable. What the existing 
literature does not provide is a systematic account of which legitimation strategies appear in the UNGDC 
corpus, in what combinations, with what frequency, and how the distribution has shifted across the 
postwar period. The published research consists of deep vertical borings into particular episodes; the 
horizontal view – across states, across time, across violation types – remains unavailable. This study 
addresses that gap.

2. Theoretical Framework 
2.1. Habermas’s Theory of Communicative Action: Core Tenets and Typology 
The theoretical foundation of this study rests on Jürgen Habermas’s theory of communicative 
action, developed in The Theory of Communicative Action, first published in German in 1981. Habermas’s 
project was animated by dissatisfaction with accounts of rationality that reduced it to instrumental and 
strategic reason, oriented toward the efficient pursuit of pre-given ends. Against this, he argued that a 
second form of rationality – communicative rationality – is embedded in the structures of language use 
and oriented not toward success but toward mutual understanding (Verständigung). This shift from a 
subject-object to a subject-subject paradigm is the conceptual foundation on which his typology of social 
action rests. 
In Volume 1, Chapter I, Habermas distinguishes four concepts of social action. Teleological action 
– and, when it incorporates other actors as part of the strategic calculus, strategic action – is oriented 
toward the attainment of an end through the selection of appropriate means. Normatively regulated action 
refers to behavior governed by shared group values; in later institutionalist terms, it approximates the logic 
of appropriateness. Dramaturgical action involves the self-presentation of an actor before an audience, the 
management of impressions in a social performance. Communicative action is the fourth and analytically 
central type: action coordinated through language oriented toward reaching intersubjective agreement, in 
which actors seek to harmonize their plans not through incentives, threats, or norm compliance alone, but 
by advancing claims that are open to reasoned acceptance or rejection. As Habermas puts it, “the 
communicative model of action does not equate action with communication. Language is a means of 
communication which serves mutual understanding, whereas actors, in coming to an understanding with 
one another so as to coordinate their actions, pursue their particular aims” (Habermas, 1984, p. 101). 
Following the IR applications of Habermas developed by Risse and Bjola, this study focuses on 
three validity claims most relevant to political justification. Truth concerns whether the propositional 
content corresponds to states of affairs in the objective world. Rightness concerns whether the utterance is 
normatively appropriate within a shared social world and whether the underlying norm can be justified. 
Sincerity concerns whether the expression corresponds to the speaker’s actual intentions. These claims 
carry the expectation that they could, in principle, be defended discursively if challenged. The 
communicative model treats speech not as the mere transmission of information but as a form of social 
action in which coordination depends on the intersubjective recognition of validity. 
The distinction between communicative and strategic action is the analytical axis most relevant to 
this study. Strategic action is oriented toward success (Erfolg) – the achievement of the actor’s own 
 

--- PAGE 15 ---
15 
objectives regardless of others’ genuine agreement; communicative action is oriented toward 
understanding (Verständigung) – a shared assessment of a situation arrived at through reasons that all 
parties can in principle accept. Habermas identifies a degenerate but empirically pervasive phenomenon 
he terms systematically distorted communication: strategic action dressed in the grammar of 
communicative exchange, deploying the surface forms of reasoned argument while remaining, at the level 
of orientation, purely strategic. This tension is especially relevant for UNGA speeches, where heads of 
government may justify legally contested conduct before a universal audience in the language of reasoned 
argument, even when the purpose is reputational management rather than genuine argumentative revision. 
2.2. Communicative Action in International Relations 
The application of Habermas’s theory to world politics required a deliberate theoretical translation. 
Much of mainstream IR theory, particularly rationalist and realist work, had long been organized around 
interest-based models – rational choice bargaining, deterrence theory, the strategic logic of great-power 
competition – that treated communication as epiphenomenal to power and interest. The constructivist turn 
of the 1990s brought normative factors back into analytical view, but the specific question of 
argumentation as a mode of social action required a further step. 
Thomas Risse (2000) made that step in a widely cited contribution. Against the bilateral opposition 
between consequentialist rationalism and constructivist appropriateness, Risse proposed a third mode of 
social interaction: the logic of arguing. “Processes of argumentation, deliberation, and persuasion,” he 
contended, “constitute a distinct mode of social interaction to be differentiated from both strategic 
bargaining…and rule-guided behavior” (Risse, 2000, p. 1). Actors engaged in arguing advance truth 
claims or normative validity claims with a genuine openness to revising their positions in light of better 
arguments – which is what distinguishes arguing from bargaining (adjusting positions through promises 
and threats) and from rhetorical action (deploying the forms of argument without readiness for revision) 
(Risse, 2000). Risse distinguishes between two settings in which argumentative processes operate in 
international relations: restricted diplomatic exchanges among small numbers of participants, where actors 
may undergo genuine shifts in their stated preferences, is at least conceivable; and open, public discourses 
addressed to wider audiences where argumentative accountability operates at a different scale. The UNGA 
General Debate belongs to the second category – its institutionalized, universally public character makes it 
a site where states must advance normative justifications subject, in principle, to scrutiny from all 193 
member states. Whether that scrutiny produces genuine arguing or merely a strategic performance of 
argument is precisely the empirical question this study examines. 
 

--- PAGE 16 ---
16 
Harald Müller (2004) introduced an important conceptual refinement to Risse’s framework. Risse 
had treated “arguing” and “communicative action” as roughly synonymous – both contrasted with 
bargaining and strategic action. Müller showed that this collapsed a critical distinction: “arguing and 
bargaining are simply two different types of speech acts… Communicative and strategic action, in 
contrast, are not speech acts, but types of action that include the wider orientation of the speakers” 
(Müller, 2004, p. 396). The consequence is that strategic actors can and do use arguing speech acts, 
deploying the form of reasoned justification instrumentally without any genuine openness to 
argumentative revision. As Müller observes, “Jon Elster (1991) has shown how strategic actors would use 
arguing for winning over their partners to their own preferences if they believe that arguments are a better 
way to win than promises and threats” (Müller, 2004, p. 397). For this study, the distinction is analytically 
essential: the presence of arguing speech acts in UNGA addresses does not establish that the speaker is 
engaged in communicative action. The coding framework, developed in Section 2.3 below, therefore 
tracks strategies – types of speech act – separately from the question of orientation, which is addressed in 
the interpretive analysis of Chapter 4. 
Corneliu Bjola (2005) offered the most direct bridge between the Habermasian framework and the 
analysis of legitimation in diplomatic practice. Bjola argued that legal compliance criteria – specifically, 
UNSC authorization – are insufficient to distinguish legitimate from illegitimate uses of force, because 
legal standards can themselves become objects of strategic manipulation. He proposed instead the concept 
of deliberative legitimacy, defined as “the non-coerced commitment of an actor to obey a norm adopted on 
the basis of the criteria and rules reached through a process of communicative action” (Bjola, 2005, 
p. 266). The operative question is whether a discourse is genuinely oriented toward building a reasoned 
consensus or whether it performs the grammar of argument while remaining structurally closed to 
outcome: “Do the promoters of military interventions try to reach a reasoned consensus on the need for 
the use of force, or do they just engage in power games based on credible threats, intimidation or 
rhetorical exchanges with no visible intention to achieve argumentative consensus?” (Bjola, 2005, p. 267). 
Applying this distinction to the Kosovo and Iraq cases, Bjola found that NATO’s deliberative process prior 
to 1999 exhibited at least some features of communicative action – positions shifted in response to 
arguments advanced within the alliance – while the Bush administration’s management of the Security 
Council process in 2002–2003 was characterized by strategic action: legal language (Resolution 1441) 
used to construct cover for a decision that was not open to argumentative revision (Bjola, 2005). 
Naomi Head (2008) extended this analysis toward the procedural conditions under which 
legitimating discourse can be considered genuinely communicative. Drawing on Habermas’s concept of 
 

--- PAGE 17 ---
17 
the ideal speech situation – defined by equal standing to initiate and participate in discourse, the right to 
challenge claims, and the absence of coercion – Head identified patterns of structural exclusion that distort 
apparently deliberative processes even when they occur in formally multilateral settings. “Habermas 
develops the ideal speech situation in order to be able to distinguish genuine communication from false or 
pseudo-communication,” she observes (Head, 2008, p. 152). In the Kosovo case, the exclusion of Kosovo 
Albanians from Security Council deliberations and the coercive framing of the Rambouillet negotiations 
meant that even the procedural forms of dialogue failed to satisfy minimal conditions of non-distorted 
communication (Head, 2008). For this study, Head’s analysis points toward a structural feature of UNGA 
speeches that bears on their interpretation: states delivering legitimation addresses before the General 
Assembly are not participants in an equal deliberative exchange – they occupy a structurally marked 
position within an asymmetric communicative space, defending conduct that is already the subject of 
public contestation. 
The synthesis these four contributions enable can be stated as follows. State leaders addressing the 
UNGA in the wake of legally contested actions face a situation in which the validity claim of rightness – 
the normative appropriateness of their conduct – is under explicit challenge. They must respond publicly, 
before a universal audience, and in a genre that carries the institutional expectation of normative 
justification. The theoretical expectation – grounded in Habermas’s typology, Risse’s distinction between 
arguing and rhetorical action, Müller’s speech-act correction, and Bjola’s deliberative legitimacy 
framework – is that the dominant mode will be strategic action in communicative form: arguing speech 
acts deployed without genuine openness to argumentative revision. Verifying that expectation, and 
mapping the specific strategies through which it is realized, is the task of Chapters 3 and 4. 
2.3. Operationalizing Legitimation in Diplomatic Discourse 
Converting the theoretical framework into an analytical instrument requires specifying what is to 
be observed in UNGA speech texts. Legitimation, as used in this study, refers to the public construction of 
normative justifications for conduct whose legality or normative acceptability is publicly disputed. This 
definition draws on Gronau and Schmidtke’s (2016) characterization of legitimation as a goal-oriented 
practice and connects it to the Habermasian account of validity claims – specifically the claim of rightness 
– as the dimension of speech at stake when states seek to defend contested actions before international 
audiences. 
The coding framework operationalizes legitimation along two analytical dimensions. The first 
captures the type of alleged violation that the speech is responding to: (1) use of force and jus ad bellum 
allegations (contested resort to armed force); (2) IHL allegations (conduct alleged to violate the laws of 
 

--- PAGE 18 ---
18 
armed conflict); (3) human rights allegations (domestic repression, mass atrocities, persecution); (4) 
territorial integrity and sovereignty claims (annexation, occupation, contested secession); (5) treaty and 
UNSC-resolution non-compliance. These categories are not mutually exclusive – a single address may 
respond to overlapping allegations – but they provide the primary contextual variable against which the 
choice of legitimation strategy can be assessed. 
The second dimension captures the legitimation strategy itself: the discursive move through which 
the speaker constructs normative cover for the contested action. Eight strategy types are distinguished: (1) 
self-defense and necessity claims – a cluster of defensive justifications encompassing both the legal right 
of self-defense under Art. 51 of the UN Charter and broader appeals to necessity (security, political, or 
humanitarian) that frame the action as unavoidable given the circumstances; (2) sovereignty and 
non-interference – characterization of external critique as illegitimate intervention in domestic affairs; (3) 
denial and reinterpretation – factual denial of the alleged conduct or its redescription under a 
non-violating frame; (4) moral and humanitarian framing – positioning the action as morally obligatory or 
as the lesser harm, including humanitarian justifications that invoke the protection of civilians or 
endangered populations; (5) counter-terrorism and securitization framing – invocation of exceptional 
threat, terrorist designation, or civilizational danger to justify departure from ordinary legal standards; (6) 
whataboutism and tu quoque – deflection through accusation of the accuser or generalization of the 
alleged violation as universal; (7) procedural-legal claims – appeals to formal procedural compliance, 
treaty authority, or multilateral authorization; (8) historical and existential narratives – justification 
grounded in historical grievance, territorial-historical claims, or identity-based existential framing. 
The cross-tabulation of these two dimensions – violation type against strategy type – generates the 
analytical matrix for the empirical chapter. Descriptive frequencies identify which strategies are most 
common within the selected sample and which cluster around particular violation types, providing a basis 
for assessing the two hypotheses stated in Section 2.4. The qualitative comparative analysis in Chapter 4 
then examines how the same strategies manifest differently across actors, periods, and normative contexts, 
and what that variation reveals about the communicative structure of legitimation at the UNGA. 
2.4. Research Hypotheses 
On the basis of the theoretical framework developed in Sections 2.1–2.3 and the literature 
reviewed in Chapter 1, four hypotheses are advanced for empirical testing in Chapter 4. 
H1 (ideational convergence). Post-9/11 counter-terrorism framing increases uniformly across all 
geopolitical blocs with no statistically significant inter-bloc differentiation – a pattern consistent with 
ideational convergence rather than divergence. The theoretical basis is twofold. First, UNSC Resolution 
 

--- PAGE 19 ---
19 
1373 (2001) formally established counter-terrorism as a universal obligation, making securitization 
framing available to all state actors regardless of their underlying normative position. Second, Wiener’s 
(2014) theory of contestation predicts that a norm-generative event of sufficient salience – here, the 
institutionalization of the counter-terrorism framework – can reset the available repertoire of legitimation 
resources across the international system, displacing prior bloc-specific patterns. This hypothesis is 
falsifiable: it would be rejected if one bloc showed systematically greater uptake of counter-terrorism 
language than others in the post-2001 period. 
H2 (repertoire). The dominant legitimation strategies across the corpus will function as 
normative-legal action in Habermas’s (1984) typology – oriented toward shared legal frameworks and 
validity claims of rightness – rather than as purely strategic evasion or denial. The theoretical derivation 
runs as follows. Bjola (2005) and Müller (2004) establish that states in institutionalized multilateral 
settings deploy arguing speech acts even when their underlying orientation is strategic; the forum structure 
generates a normative performance requirement. In the UNGA’s institutional setting – public, universal, 
founded on the Charter architecture – states face strong incentives to construct justifications grounded in 
shared legal standards, because purely strategic speech acts (denial, counter-accusation, delegitimation) 
forfeit the legitimating function that public justification is meant to serve. The hypothesis is falsifiable: it 
would be rejected if strategic action – as defined by the Habermas typology – constituted the dominant 
mode across the sample. 
H3 (temporal shift). The distribution of legitimation strategies shifts significantly across historical 
periods, rather than remaining stable across the 77-year period of analysis. Specifically: normative-legal 
framing is predicted to dominate during the Cold War; communicative justification – particularly 
counter-terrorism framing – to surge in the post-9/11 period; and sovereignty-revisionist normative 
framing to intensify after 2014, consistent with the fragmentation of the post-Cold War normative 
consensus documented in recent IR scholarship. The theoretical basis is Wiener’s (2014) norm 
contestation framework: if norms are continuously contested and their meaning is reproduced through 
practice, then major ruptures in the normative environment – the end of the Cold War, the post-9/11 
security turn, the post-2014 fragmentation – should produce observable shifts in the repertoire of 
legitimation resources that states can plausibly deploy. The hypothesis is falsifiable: it would be rejected if 
the chi-square test across periods yielded a non-significant result. 
H4 (geopolitical differentiation). Legitimation strategies differ systematically across Cold War 
blocs, with Western states showing a lower reliance on normative-legal framing relative to Soviet/Eastern 
and Non-Aligned states – a divergence observable despite the surface convergence captured in H1. The 
 

--- PAGE 20 ---
20 
theoretical derivation follows from the structural account in Section 2.3: if legitimation strategies are 
shaped by the normative standards that a speaker’s core audience accepts as legitimate grounds, then bloc 
membership – as a proxy for shared normative context – should predict rhetorical patterns. Western states 
operating outside the Security Council authorization framework face a structural disincentive against 
formal Charter invocations; Soviet/Eastern and Non-Aligned states, whose contested conduct typically 
concerns the exercise of domestic sovereign authority, have stronger incentives to invoke Charter 
provisions on sovereignty and non-interference. This structural asymmetry – not strategic preference – is 
predicted to generate the observable register divergence. The hypothesis is falsifiable: it would be rejected 
if the chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests across blocs yielded non-significant results. 
All four hypotheses are tested in Chapter 4 against the 33-case sample. Their results are interpreted 
in Chapter 5.

3. Methodology 
3.1. Research Design: A Two-Level Analytical Strategy 
The analytical approach adopted in this study combines two complementary levels of analysis, 
each designed to answer a distinct set of research questions and together enabling both breadth and 
interpretive depth that neither level could achieve alone. 
The first level is a corpus-level keyword signal analysis, applied to a broad sample of speeches 
drawn from the full Harvard UN General Debate Corpus (UNGDC). This level is designed to address the 
macro-questions motivating the study: When does the frequency of particular legitimation-related 
linguistic signals increase or decrease across time? Are these changes associated with geopolitical bloc 
membership, historical period, or the profile of actors involved in the discourse? The corpus-level analysis 
does not code individual arguments; it tracks the normalized keyword density of seven thematic signals – 
counter-terrorism and securitization framing, self-defense appeals, sovereignty invocations, humanitarian 
discourse, procedural-legal references, historical framing, and whataboutism – across over 11,000 
speeches, disaggregated by Cold War bloc membership and nine sub-periods: 1946–1959, 1960–1969, 
1970–1979, 1980–1989, 1990–2001, 2002–2008, 2009–2013, 2014–2019, and 2020–2023. These nine 
sub-periods serve exclusively as the temporal grid for corpus-level signal trend analysis and are distinct 
from the four conceptually defined historical periods used for case-level coding in Section 3.4.3. Where 
the nine sub-periods are approximately decadal intervals chosen to ensure adequate speech volume per 
cell, the four analytical periods in Section 3.4.3 are defined by geopolitical inflection points (the end of the 
Cold War, the post-9/11 security turn, and the post-2014 normative fragmentation) and serve as the 
interpretive frame for the case-level comparative analysis. This level is the primary evidentiary basis for 
testing H1 (convergence) and provides contextual grounding for the temporal patterns examined in H3. 
The second level is a directed qualitative content analysis of 33 case-years identified through the 
criterion-based sampling procedure described in Section 3.3. This level engages the 102 coded 
argumentative segments from those cases and classifies each according to Habermas’s (1984) four-type 
taxonomy of social action. This level is the primary evidentiary basis for testing H2, H3, and H4, and 
generates the comparative case analysis in Chapter 4. 
The rationale for this two-level design is directly tied to the gap identified in Chapter 1. Existing 
research consists predominantly of case studies that yield analytic depth but cannot speak to patterns 
across actors, periods, and violation types (Bjola, 2005; Rapp, 2022). What the literature lacks is a 
horizontal account: which strategies are deployed, with what frequency, in what combinations, and how 
the distribution has shifted over time. The corpus-level analysis provides the horizontal account at scale; 
 

--- PAGE 22 ---
22 
the case-level analysis provides the interpretive depth necessary to understand the rhetorical logic of 
individual legitimation moves. The two levels are designed to be mutually illuminating rather than 
redundant. 
Within the second level, the analytical procedure unfolds in two stages. The first stage is 
descriptive mapping: classifying all segments in the sample against the Habermas typology, calculating 
frequencies and co-occurrence patterns, and generating the quantitative findings reported in Sections 
4.3–4.6. The second stage is comparative qualitative interpretation: examining in closer detail how 
specific legitimation strategies are realized in representative cases – which rhetorical resources are 
deployed, which legal and normative frameworks are invoked, and how the framing responds to the 
specific allegation addressed. As Hsieh and Shannon (2005) argue in their account of directed content 
analysis, this combination of deductive category-driven coding with qualitative interpretation allows a 
study to move between the pattern level and the meaning level in a principled way. 
The unit of analysis is the argumentative segment – a sentence or sequence of sentences 
constituting a rhetorically complete justificatory move. A single speech may contain multiple coded 
segments, each classified independently, since states defending contested conduct typically assemble 
composite justifications rather than relying on a single argument. 
3.2. Data: The Harvard UN General Debate Corpus 
The primary data source is the UN General Debate Corpus (UNGDC). The original corpus was 
compiled from UN official records and published on Harvard Dataverse by Baturo, Dasandi, and 
Mikhaylov (2017), who made available the full texts of speeches delivered during the General Debate of 
the United Nations General Assembly for sessions spanning 1970 to 2014, as a structured text-as-data 
resource. The extended version used in this study was developed by Jankin, Baturo, and Dasandi (2025), 
who expanded coverage backward to the inaugural General Assembly session in 1946 and forward 
through 2023. As employed in the present study, the corpus encompasses 11,127 speeches from 198 
countries across nearly eight decades of General Debate sessions. 
The corpus offers several properties that make it suitable for both levels of the analysis. Speeches 
are available as plain-text files, machine-readable and standardized in format, with consistent metadata on 
the year of delivery, country, and session number. The General Debate format is highly uniform across 
time: each member state receives a designated speaking slot, delivers an address on topics of its choosing 
before a plenary audience that includes the full UN membership. As Baturo et al. (2017, p. 3) observe, this 
genre stability – combined with the fact that “member states present themselves exclusively in the guise in 
which they wish to be known” – makes the corpus particularly well-suited to the systematic analysis of 
 

--- PAGE 23 ---
23 
strategic legitimation discourse. Rather than treating strategic self-presentation as a distortion to be 
corrected, this study takes it as its primary analytical object. 
The corpus-level analysis draws on the full UNGDC for signal frequency computation. The 
case-level analysis draws on 33 state-years identified through the criterion-based procedure described 
below. Python scripts serve three preparatory functions: extracting speeches for identified state-years; 
applying the keyword-based role classifier to all segments; and generating the corpus-level keyword 
signal scores used for ANOVA and temporal trend analysis. 
Two limitations of the corpus require acknowledgment. First, speeches originally delivered in 
languages other than English are represented through official UN translations. The translation process 
introduces the possibility that strategic nuances in the original are smoothed out or lost, which the 
qualitative interpretive stage notes where salient. Second, speeches prior to approximately 1992 were 
stored as scanned copies of printed records and required OCR processing, which may affect text quality 
for some early sessions (Baturo et al., 2017; Jankin et al., 2025). 
3.3. Case Identification and Geopolitical Classification 
3.3.1. Criterion-Based Case Selection 
The analytical sample for the case-level analysis includes all speeches in the UNGDC that satisfy 
the study’s inclusion criterion: the delivering state faced documented external criticism or allegation of 
legally or normatively contested conduct at the time of the relevant UNGA session, and the speech itself 
contains material – direct or indirect – addressing that contested conduct. No predetermined N constrains 
the sample; the inclusion criterion is applied systematically to the full corpus. The present study identifies 
33 state-years meeting both conditions, spanning 18 states across all four analytical periods (Cold War: 8 
cases; post-Cold War normative expansion: 9; post-9/11 security turn: 8; post-2014 normative 
fragmentation: 8). 
Case identification proceeds in two steps. First, episodes of legally or normatively contested 
conduct are identified using contextual documentation: UN reporting (General Assembly and Security 
Council records, special rapporteur reports), NGO documentation (Amnesty International, Human Rights 
Watch annual reports), and academic secondary literature on specific conflicts. These sources document 
that a state faced external criticism at the time of its UNGA address; they do not establish that a legal 
violation occurred, and no claim about the legality of the conduct is made. Second, identified state-years 
are checked against the UNGDC to confirm that the speech for that country and session is available and 
contains material relevant to the contested episode. Speeches that make no reference – direct or indirect – 
to the contested conduct are excluded.

3.3.2. Cold War Bloc Classification 
The geopolitical comparative dimension of the analysis is structured around three Cold War 
institutional blocs: the Western bloc (NATO member states and their formal allies), the Soviet/Eastern 
bloc (Warsaw Pact member states and Soviet-aligned governments), and the Non-Aligned Movement 
(NAM member states and formally uncommitted governments). The choice of this classification over 
alternative schemes – UN regional groups, regime type classifications, or contemporary alliance 
configurations – requires explicit justification. 
The Cold War bloc classification is most appropriate for the temporal scope of this study for three 
interconnected reasons. First, the study covers 77 years of UN General Assembly discourse (1946–2023), 
the majority of which – roughly 44 years (1946–1989) – falls within the predominantly bipolar 
international system shaped by the Cold War. During this period, bloc membership was not merely a 
diplomatic affiliation but a marker of shared normative contexts, overlapping legal frameworks, and 
distinctive vocabularies of legitimate international conduct. Western bloc states operated within a liberal 
internationalist normative framework emphasizing rule-based order and human rights conditionality; 
Soviet/Eastern bloc states prioritized formal sovereignty and non-interference as counterweights to 
Western interventionism; Non-Aligned states, shaped by the Bandung tradition, decolonization, and 
South–South diplomatic coordination, constructed a third normative repertoire emphasizing 
anti-colonialism, sovereign equality, non-interference, and self-determination (Prashad, 2007; Dinkel, 
2019). If legitimation strategies are shaped by the normative standards that a speaker’s core audience 
accepts as legitimate grounds – which the Habermasian framework would predict – then bloc membership 
is the analytically relevant dimension. 
Second, the bloc structure remains institutionally stable across the full period, unlike post-Cold 
War alliance configurations or economic groupings which have shifted substantially. The core 
composition of the Western and Soviet/Eastern blocs remained stable from the early 1950s through 1991. 
The Non-Aligned Movement, while more heterogeneous, maintained a broadly consistent normative 
orientation from its founding. This stability allows meaningful temporal comparison across all four 
analytical periods without requiring recoding of states’ positions. 
Third, the only structural disruption – the entry of fourteen Eastern European states into NATO 
after 1991 – is handled through a period-aware transition rule (BLOC_TRANSITIONS): states retain their 
Soviet/Eastern bloc classification through their year of NATO accession, after which they are reclassified 
as Western bloc. The states affected by this rule (including Bulgaria, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, 
Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia – 2004; Albania, Croatia – 2009; Montenegro – 
 

--- PAGE 25 ---
25 
2017; North Macedonia – 2020) do not appear in the 33-case sample, so the transition rule does not affect 
the reported results. Its inclusion preserves conceptual integrity and relevance for any expanded analysis. 
Whether blocs can be shown to differ in their legitimation repertoires is precisely what the 
statistical comparisons in Chapter 4 test. The bloc classification is not assumed to produce differences; it 
is a theoretically motivated hypothesis that the data either support or reject. 
3.4. Coding Framework 
3.4.1. Habermas’s Typology as the Master Coding Architecture 
The case-level analysis classifies each coded segment according to Habermas’s (1984) four-type 
taxonomy of social action. The decision to apply this deductively derived framework rather than 
developing categories inductively from the corpus follows the logic of directed qualitative content 
analysis: when existing theory provides sufficiently specified categories, it is both methodologically more 
defensible and analytically more productive to test those categories against new data rather than 
reinventing a descriptive vocabulary (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). Prior IR scholarship supports the 
applicability of Habermas’s typology to multilateral diplomatic discourse. Risse (2000) demonstrates that 
the distinction between arguing and bargaining – which maps directly onto communicative versus 
strategic action in Habermas’s framework – generates empirically testable predictions about actor 
behavior in international institutions. Müller (2004) extends this by showing that the distinction operates 
at the level of actor orientation (toward understanding vs. toward success) rather than at the level of 
specific speech acts, which means that the same argumentative form can be communicative or strategic 
depending on the speaker’s underlying orientation toward the audience. Bjola (2005) applies the 
framework directly to UNGA-adjacent discourse, demonstrating its purchase on the analysis of how states 
construct deliberative legitimacy for contested uses of force.

treated as complementary rather than redundant in the empirical analysis.

3.5. Statistical Methods 
The quantitative dimension of the analysis employs a suite of statistical methods, selected to match 
the data structure (categorical, small-N, often with sparse cell frequencies) and the substantive questions 
being addressed.

statistic measures the deviation of observed cell frequencies from what would be expected if the two 
categorical variables were independent. Effect size is reported as Cramér’s V, which normalizes chi-square 
for table dimensions and sample size. Given the small expected cell frequencies characteristic of the 
33-case sample (many cells below the conventional threshold of 5), all chi-square results are accompanied 
by the percentage of cells with expected frequency below 5, and results with high proportions of such cells 
are interpreted with explicit caution. 
Fisher’s exact test is used for pairwise bloc comparisons, where the small sample sizes render 
chi-square approximations unreliable. Fisher’s exact test computes the exact probability of observing the 
given contingency table or a more extreme one under the null hypothesis of independence, without relying 
on large-sample approximations. It is preferred over chi-square for 2×2 and small contingency tables with 
expected cell counts below 5. 
Adjusted standardized residuals are used to identify which specific role-bloc combinations deviate 
most substantially from expected frequencies. Unlike raw residuals, adjusted residuals account for both 
the marginal totals of the row and column and the overall sample size, making comparisons across cells in 
the same table meaningful. Values exceeding ±2 are interpreted as indicating statistically notable over- or 
under-representation. 
Correspondence analysis (CA) provides a geometric representation of the association structure in a 
contingency table. CA decomposes the chi-square distance between row and column profiles via singular 
value decomposition (SVD), producing principal coordinates for both rows (communicative roles) and 
columns (blocs or periods) in a low-dimensional space. The proportion of total inertia (analogous to 
variance) captured by each dimension is reported via scree plots; dimensions capturing cumulatively more 
than 80 percent of total inertia are treated as the substantively meaningful space. CA was implemented via 
SVD in Python (numpy) without external dimensionality-reduction libraries, ensuring full procedural 
transparency.

One-way ANOVA is used to test whether the mean corpus-level keyword signal score for the 
counter-terrorism dimension differs significantly across the three blocs in the pre-2001 and post-2001 
periods separately. ANOVA tests the null hypothesis that group means are equal; a non-significant result 
(both pre- and post-2001) is the predicted outcome under H1, indicating that adoption of counter-terrorism 
framing did not cluster in a single bloc. 
Two-sample t-tests (independent samples) are used to assess whether the mean counter-terrorism 
signal score differs significantly between the pre-2001 and post-2001 periods within each bloc. This tests 
whether the post-9/11 shift detected in the case-level analysis is also present at the corpus level, across all 
bloc members – not only those in the 33-case sample.

Reliability is addressed through double-coding: a subset of the sample (approximately 20 percent 
of speeches) is independently coded by a trained research assistant working from the same codebook.

Validity in directed content analysis depends on whether the coding categories adequately capture 
the phenomenon of interest. Construct validity is grounded in the theoretical derivation of the categories 
from Habermas (1984), operationalized through the IR applications in Risse (2000), Müller (2004), and 
Bjola (2005). Face validity is assessed through the pilot phase: if trained coders systematically struggle to 
assign segments to categories, this signals that category definitions require refinement. 
Claims are appropriately bounded by the study’s design. The analysis identifies and describes the 
distribution of legitimation strategies within the selected sample of speeches from states facing allegations 
of contested conduct; it does not claim that this distribution is representative of all UNGA speeches. The 
temporal dimension captures distributional shifts across the sample; no causal mechanisms are 
established. No adjudicative claims are made: coding a segment as deploying self-defense framing is not a 
finding that the state’s action was or was not lawful. The object of analysis is justificatory discourse – how 
states publicly account for their conduct – not the legal or factual merits of those accounts. 
Two further constraints apply. The corpus covers only the UNGA General Debate; legitimation 
strategies operative in the Security Council, bilateral diplomatic communications, or domestic political 
discourse fall outside the scope of analysis. Translation from non-English originals introduces the risk of

4.2. Analytical Architecture. Operationalization and Methodological Rationale 
4.2.1. Habermas’s Typology as a Coding Framework 
The decision to apply Habermas’s (1984) four-type taxonomy of social action – communicative, 
normative, dramaturgical, and strategic – as the master framework for classifying coded segments, rather 
than working from an inductively derived set of categories, follows the logic of directed qualitative 
content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). The theory provides the initial categorization structure; the 
 

--- PAGE 34 ---
34 
data refine it. This approach is defensible here because the theoretical categories are sufficiently specified 
at the conceptual level to generate operational distinctions, and because prior IR scholarship has 
consistently found Habermas’s typology applicable to multilateral diplomatic discourse (Risse, 2000; 
Bjola, 2005; Müller, 2004). 
The mapping from nine communicative roles to four Habermas action types was constructed 
deductively: 
●​
Communicative action (oriented toward Verständigung, reaching understanding through 
reasons). Covers the roles of justification (presenting reasons why the action was necessary or defensible) 
and dialogue_appeal (directly seeking interlocutors’ reasoned engagement). These roles share the validity 
claim of truth – the speaker presents propositional content as factually or normatively accurate and invites 
rational assessment; 
●​
Normative action (oriented toward Richtigkeit, norm-conformity). Covers legal_normative 
(explicit invocation of international legal instruments – the UN Charter, UNSC resolutions, treaty 
provisions) and sovereignty_norm (appeals to the principle of non-interference as a binding norm). These 
roles assert rightness – the speaker positions the action as consistent with a binding normative standard 
shared by the audience; 
●​
Dramaturgical action (oriented toward Wahrhaftigkeit, self-presentation as authentic). 
Covers historical_context (contextualizing the action within a narrative of prior injustice or historical 
claim) and victim_narrative (presenting the state as the injured party). These roles stage sincerity – the 
speaker constructs a presentation of the situation that positions the self’s motives as genuine and the action 
as emotionally and morally comprehensible; 
●​
Strategic action (oriented toward success, not mutual understanding): covers denial, 
counter_accusation, and delegitimation. These roles involve no claim to shared normative ground; the 
speaker moves to remove the accusation rather than address it. 
This four-part structure permits the analysis to ask not only which strategies appear, but what kind 
of legitimation work they perform – whether states construct shared reasons, invoke shared norms, display 
authentic self-presentation, or attempt to shut down the legitimation game entirely. 
4.2.2. Cold War Bloc Construction and Block Transitions 
The choice to structure the geopolitical comparison around Cold War institutional blocs – rather 
than, for instance, UN regional groups, regime type classifications, or contemporary alliance memberships 
– requires explicit justification. 
 

--- PAGE 35 ---
35 
Cold War bloc membership reflects shared institutional embeddedness during the period in which 
the foundational normative architecture of the postwar international order was being constructed and 
contested. States aligned with NATO and the Western alliance not only shared security arrangements but 
also participated in overlapping legal, economic, and diplomatic institutions that shaped a common 
normative vocabulary – one oriented toward liberal rules-based frameworks and, in later decades, human 
rights conditionality. States of the Soviet and Eastern bloc operated within a distinct normative context 
that privileged formal sovereignty, non-interference, and the legal primacy of the UN Charter as a check 
on Western interventionism. Non-Aligned Movement states, particularly through the Bandung framework 
and subsequent Group of 77 solidarities, constructed a third normative repertoire oriented simultaneously 
toward formal legal equality, anti-colonialism, and sovereignty protection (Prashad, 2007; Dinkel, 2019). 
If legitimation strategies are shaped by normative context – by the standards of rightness that a 
speaker’s core audience treats as legitimate grounds – then bloc membership during the Cold War period 
should predict rhetorical patterns in ways that post-Cold War alliance configurations or regional groupings 
would not capture as cleanly. 
A technical complication arises from the entry of fourteen Eastern European states into NATO 
after 1991. Treating Poland, Hungary, or Estonia as part of the Soviet/Eastern bloc in 2004 would 
misrepresent their normative affiliation. The analysis therefore implements period-aware bloc transitions: 
states retain their Soviet/Eastern bloc classification up to their year of NATO accession, after which they 
are reclassified as Western bloc members. Since none of the 14 transitioning states appear as case-years in 
the analytical sample, this coding rule does not affect the results reported here – but it preserves 
conceptual integrity and would matter significantly in any expanded analysis of the post-1999 Eastern 
European discourse. 
4.2.3. Keyword Scoring and the Unclassified Segment Rate 
Each coded segment is scored by computing keyword hit density across nine role-specific lexicons 
(hits per 1,000 words). The role with the highest non-zero score is assigned as the dominant role for that 
segment; segments with all-zero scores across all lexicons are designated unclassified. The overall 
unclassified rate is 4.9 percent. Within the Western bloc, the rate is notably higher at 11.1 percent (three 
segments from the nine Western cases). This asymmetry is analytically informative rather than merely 
methodological: Western diplomatic discourse, particularly from the United States and Israel, frequently 
employs more institutionally indirect and hedged language than the more direct sovereignty or legal 
invocations characteristic of Soviet/Eastern and Non-Aligned speeches. A segment that constructs a 
justificatory argument through elaborate subordinate clauses and conditional framings may be harder to 
 

--- PAGE 36 ---
36 
classify by keyword frequency than one that straightforwardly asserts non-interference. The higher 
unclassified rate in the Western subcorpus can therefore be read as a trace of a different rhetorical register 
– one that operates through implication and institutional framing rather than explicit lexical assertion.

4.4. Comparative Case Analysis 
The quantitative mapping of communicative roles and action types identifies aggregate patterns; 
this section examines the rhetorical logic underlying the three most prevalent strategy types through close 
reading of representative cases. The cases were selected to illustrate how the dominant modes of 
legitimation are realized in practice – and to show that the aggregate category labels correspond to 
coherent argumentative strategies, not merely keyword co-occurrence. 
4.4.1. Self-Defense, Necessity, and the Counter-Terrorism Frame 
The most common legitimation pattern across the corpus is what the coding framework captures as 
justification – a communicative action mode in which the speaker constructs a case for the necessity, 
proportionality, or defensive character of the contested action through propositional argument rather than 
 

--- PAGE 40 ---
40 
pure norm invocation. This communicative mode dominates the Western bloc (19 of 24 classified Western 
segments, 79.2 percent) and is also the single most frequent type in the Soviet/Eastern bloc (19 of 38 
classified segments, 50.0 percent). 
The rhetorical structure of justification-dominant speeches follows a recognizable pattern. The 
speaker first establishes the nature and severity of the threat; then identifies the action taken as a necessary 
and proportionate response; then frames the action as consistent with the right of self-defense or collective 
security obligations, without necessarily invoking the specific Article 51 language. Turkey’s 2019 speech 
on Operation Peace Spring in northeast Syria illustrates this structure. Of the 8 coded segments, 7 were 
classified, and 6 of those 7 fall under the justification role (86 percent of classified segments). The 
argument is structured around two claims: that PKK and YPG activities across the Syrian border 
constituted an active terrorist threat to Turkish territory and population, and that Operation Peace Spring 
was a proportionate response in conformity with the right of self-defense and relevant UN Security 
Council resolutions. The word “terrorism” and its morphological variants appear at high keyword density 
throughout the relevant passages. The explicit Article 51 invocation appears only once; the burden of the 
argument is carried instead by the counter-terrorism frame. 
This pattern – necessity argument through counter-terrorism framing, with legal invocation as 
support rather than primary vehicle – appears consistently across the post-9/11 sample. Israel’s speeches 
on the Second Lebanon War (2006) and Operation Cast Lead (2009), the United States’ address following 
the Abu Ghraib allegations (2004), and Russia’s speeches on the First and Second Chechen conflicts 
(1995, 2000) all follow this architecture. Russia’s 2000 address responds to allegations of civilian harm 
during the Second Chechen campaign by setting out the government’s account of the conflict: 83 percent 
of its classified content falls in the justification role, presenting the operation as a necessary 
counter-terrorism response to internationally organized armed groups and citing the post-1999 Chechen 
bombings as the immediate precipitating context that rendered the action unavoidable. In this sense, the 
discursive logic of the “war on terror” was already operationally present in Russian UNGA rhetoric before 
September 2001, which the post-9/11 institutionalization of counter-terrorism discourse subsequently 
extended to a much wider range of actors. Figure 4.5 summarizes the contrast between communicative

4.4.2. Normative-Legal Invocations: Charter Appeals and Sovereignty Framing 
The second major cluster – normative action – is most pronounced in the Soviet/Eastern and 
Non-Aligned Movement blocs. Of the 38 classified Soviet/Eastern segments, 15 (39.5 percent) are coded

classified segments (12.5 percent) fall under this type. 
Within the normative cluster, two sub-roles carry distinct rhetorical weight. Legal_normative 
invocations – direct citation of treaty provisions, Charter articles, or UNSC resolution language – appear 
most frequently in the Non-Aligned and Soviet/Eastern blocs: 11 and 9 segments respectively, compared

Iraq’s 1990 speech, delivered in the immediate aftermath of the intervention in Kuwait, offers a 
concentrated illustration of normative-legal framing. All three coded segments are classified as 
legal_normative (normative action type), with the speaker grounding the argument in an extended 
interpretation of UNSC resolutions, historical treaty rights, and claims about the legal status of Kuwait

constructs a legal claim. China’s 1997 speech on Tibet and Xinjiang concentrates 67 percent of its 
classified content in legal_normative and 33 percent in sovereignty_norm, producing a two-pronged 
normative defense: the matters are legally internal to Chinese sovereignty, and external commentary 
constitutes an impermissible interference. 
Vietnam’s 1980 speech on the military presence in Cambodia presents an analytically interesting

classified under sovereignty_norm, but the content of the norm invoked is the right of Cambodia to invite 
fraternal assistance – a normative claim about the legal character of the intervention rather than a 
straightforward non-interference appeal. The argument inverts the usual structure: rather than defending 
Vietnam’s conduct by asserting its own sovereignty, the speaker defends it by asserting Cambodia’s 
normative invitation. This inverted sovereignty argument is structurally similar to the Soviet defense of 
the intervention in Czechoslovakia (1968), where the legal_normative role carries a parallel argument 
about the Warsaw Pact’s collective security obligations.

4.4.3 Historical and Dramaturgical Registers 
Dramaturgical action – oriented toward constructing an authentic self-presentation rather than

The two dramaturgical roles – historical_context and victim_narrative – tend to appear in cases

state does not primarily deny the alleged conduct but claims it was a response to prior victimization, 
thereby repositioning the normative burden of justification onto the accusing parties. 
4.5. Temporal Dynamics: Strategy Evolution and the H3 Assessment 
The temporal structure of the sample – four historical periods defined by geopolitical turning 
points rather than arbitrary intervals – allows the analysis to trace whether legitimation strategies are 
historically stable or whether they shift in response to structural changes in the normative environment of 
international relations. 
The periodization is constructed as follows. The Cold War period (1946–1989) captures the 
foundational decades of the postwar order, marked by superpower rivalry and a relatively stable, if 
contested, distribution of normative authority between the UN system’s legal framework and the 
ideological blocs that operated alongside it. The Post–Cold War period (1990–2001) marks the transition 
to a normatively more fluid order in which liberal internationalism expanded its claims, humanitarian 
intervention emerged as a contested norm, and the UN Security Council became an active (if inconsistent) 
enforcement body. The Post-9/11 Security Turn (2002–2013) begins with the September 2001 attacks and 
UNSC Resolution 1373 (2001), which established counter-terrorism as a global security obligation and 
provided a new legitimation resource to a diverse range of state actors. The Post-2014 Normative 
Fragmentation period begins with Russia’s incorporation of Crimea in 2014 – which the majority of UN 
member states characterized as annexation in General Assembly resolution 68/262 – and the subsequent 
intensification of sovereignty-revisionist rhetoric – a period in which the normative consensus of the

5.2. Communicative vs. Strategic Action in Practice 
The finding that most directly speaks to the Habermasian theoretical framework – and that the 
prior case-study literature anticipated but could not establish at scale – is the complete absence of strategic 
action as a dominant communicative mode across three Cold War blocs, four historical periods, and eight 
distinct legitimation strategy types. Risse (2000) and Bjola (2005) argued theoretically that multilateral 
forums impose communicative constraints on state speech acts; the corpus-level evidence here provides 
systematic empirical confirmation of that proposition beyond the individual episodes they examined. 
Habermas (1984) distinguishes communicative action from strategic action on the criterion of 
orientation: communicative action is oriented toward Verständigung – reaching understanding through the 
exchange of reasons – while strategic action is oriented toward causal success, treating the other as a 
means rather than an interlocutor. The empirical pattern in the UNGA corpus suggests that the General 
Assembly forum imposes a structural constraint on strategic action at the textual level: every coded speech 
act, including those that colloquially might be described as deflection or counter-attack, contains sufficient 
justificatory or normative content to be classified under communicative or normative action. The forum’s 
public character, universal membership, and founding normative architecture appear to require – at 
minimum – the performance of legitimation. 
The qualification matters. Risse (2000) and Müller (2004) both observe that the distinction 
between sincere argumentation and strategic mimicry of argument is empirically difficult to establish from 
discourse data alone. A state may invoke self-defense language without any genuine orientation toward 
shared normative evaluation; the verbal form of legitimation does not guarantee its communicative 
substance. What the data establish is more modest but not trivial: states before the General Assembly 
cannot afford to appear strategically oriented. The institutional setting generates a performative demand 
for normative justification that shapes the rhetorical surface of diplomatic discourse regardless of 
underlying intent. This matches the logic of deliberative legitimacy that Bjola (2005) identifies: the 
construction of normative consensus through public argumentation operates as a constraint on state 
behavior even when states have no intention of being bound by the consensus they construct. 
The bloc-level register differentiation identified under H4 adds a further layer of theoretical 
complexity. Western states construct necessity arguments; non-Western states invoke legal norms. Both 
are forms of legitimation, but they make structurally different validity claims (Habermas, 1984): 
communicative action claims normative rightness through propositional argument about consequences and 
proportionality, while normative action claims rightness through the assertion of shared binding standards. 
The Western preference for justificatory framing over formal legal invocation plausibly reflects the 
 

--- PAGE 56 ---
56 
structural position of states whose contested actions have often been undertaken without Security Council 
authorization – a context in which formal Charter invocations would be legally and politically incoherent. 
The structural logic is documented in Section 4.6: when the formal Charter framework is unavailable, the 
substitution is quasi-legal necessity argumentation rather than an abandonment of legitimation. The 
non-Western preference for normative-legal framing, by contrast, reflects the centrality of Article 2(7) and 
formal legal equality to states whose primary exposure to international criticism concerns the exercise of 
domestic sovereign authority rather than external military conduct. 
5.3. Theoretical Contributions and Limitations 
The analysis makes three contributions to the literature on legitimation discourse in international 
politics. 
First, it provides systematic, corpus-based evidence for patterns that prior IR scholarship has 
described primarily through case analysis. Bjola (2005), Risse (2000), and Müller (2004) establish the 
relevance of communicative action to diplomatic practice through close readings of individual episodes – 
Kosovo, Iraq, the Helsinki process. The present analysis shows, across 33 case-years and 102 coded 
segments, that communicative action is not only theoretically applicable but empirically prevalent – and 
more prevalent than normative-legal invocation in the post-Cold War period. The shift to a corpus-level 
perspective does not replace the qualitative depth of case analysis; it provides the horizontal dimension 
that case studies structurally cannot. 
Second, the register differentiation finding contributes to ongoing debates about what the 
“rules-based order” means in practice. States that rhetorically champion that order invoke formal legal 
norms less frequently in cases of contested conduct than their non-Western counterparts – a pattern 
confirmed at the p < .01 level for the Western vs. Non-Aligned comparison. This is not evidence of 
hypocrisy in any simple sense; it reflects different structural exposures to the Charter architecture. 
Koskenniemi’s (2005) account of the structure of international legal argument is instructive here: legal 
argumentation oscillates between descending claims (appeals to objective norms) and ascending claims 
(appeals to state consent and practice), and states whose contested actions fall outside the formal Charter 
authorization framework face a structural disincentive against descending invocations of Charter law – 
because those very norms would render their conduct indefensible. The quasi-legal justificatory register 
characteristic of Western diplomatic argumentation in the corpus is, in this reading, a structurally 
conditioned adaptation rather than a strategic preference. The empirical pattern – non-Western states 
invoking Charter provisions and legal instruments more consistently, while Western argumentation 
operates through necessity claims and securitization framing – complicates narratives in which Western 
 

--- PAGE 57 ---
57 
states function as primary custodians of the rules-based order, pointing instead toward structural 
asymmetries that are observable in the discourse independently of any actor’s stated normative 
commitments. 
Third, the temporal finding supports the argument that September 2001 functioned as a discursive 
turning point – not merely a geopolitical one – that reconfigured the normative vocabulary available to all 
state actors regardless of strategic alignment. Wiener’s (2014) theory of contestation provides the 
theoretical framing: norm-generative events of sufficient salience can reset the available repertoire of 
legitimation resources across the international system, such that new discursive resources become 
available to actors whose prior normative positions would not ordinarily support them. The 
institutionalization of UNSC Resolution 1373’s counter-terrorism framework is precisely such an event: it 
created a legitimation resource adopted rapidly and uniformly across three blocs with widely divergent 
strategic interests. 
Two limitations constrain the scope of these contributions. The keyword-based classification is 
necessarily reductive: the nine communicative roles are operationalized through lexicons that capture 
dominant patterns while missing indirect or highly institutionalized formulations, as the elevated Western 
unclassified rate (11.1 percent) indicates. This is not a methodological failure – it is an analytically 
informative asymmetry – but it means the classification performs differentially across rhetorical registers 
and cannot fully capture the hedged, institutionally framed argumentation characteristic of 
Anglo-American diplomatic prose. The analysis also covers a single venue. Whether the legitimation 
patterns identified in General Debate speeches characterize diplomatic discourse more broadly – in the 
Security Council, in bilateral channels, in official state media – remains an open question that the UNGDC 
corpus, by design, cannot address. 
5.4. Directions for Future Research 
Three extensions appear most immediately productive. The first is a cross-venue comparison: 
systematically applying the coding framework to Security Council debates for the same state-years as the 
present sample would test whether legitimation registers shift when the audience changes from the 
universal membership of the General Assembly to the restricted great-power veto context of the Council. 
The expectation is that Security Council rhetoric would show higher normative-legal framing and reduced 
counter-terrorism argumentation – the Council’s institutional architecture incentivizes legal citation more 
directly than the General Debate format, while the absence of a universal audience reduces the rhetorical 
payoff of broad-coalition counter-terrorism framing. 
 

--- PAGE 58 ---
58 
The second extension concerns the temporal scope of the corpus itself. Pre-1956 speeches – which 
fall within the UNGDC’s coverage from Jankin, Baturo, and Dasandi (2025) but are underrepresented in 
the present sample due to sparser documentation of contested episodes – would allow assessment of 
whether Cold War normative dominance was present from the founding of the UN system or emerged 
gradually over the first decade of the organization’s operation. The hypothesis that early General 
Assembly sessions showed more explicit legal-formalist argumentation, before counter-terrorism framing 
and quasi-legal necessity arguments became available, is testable with the extended corpus. 
The third extension addresses the translation constraint. All non-English speeches in the UNGDC 
are represented through official UN translations. For the Arabic, Chinese, and Russian subcorpora – which 
together account for the majority of the Non-Aligned and Soviet/Eastern segments in the present sample – 
systematic comparison of original-language texts with their English translations would test whether the 
keyword-based classification performs equivalently across the two versions or whether the translation 
process consistently flattens strategic distinctions in ways that distort the role assignments. Such a 
comparison would be particularly important for the assessment of the H4 finding, where bloc-level 
differences in normative-legal framing are substantial and theoretically consequential. 
Conclusion 
The question driving this analysis was what legitimation strategies state leaders employ at the 
UNGA General Debate to justify legally or normatively contested conduct – which appear most 
frequently, how their distribution has shifted across seven decades of international politics, and whether 
geopolitical blocs draw on distinguishable repertoires. Examined across 33 case-years and 102 manually 
coded segments spanning 1946 to 2023, the corpus permits systematic answers to each of these questions. 
The analysis of legitimation discourse in UNGA General Debate speeches between 1946 and 2023 
yields findings that confirm three of the four hypotheses directly, and require the reformulation of H2 in a 
way that is itself a substantive conclusion. State leaders facing allegations of legally or normatively 
contested conduct do not refuse to legitimize: the complete absence of strategic action as a dominant mode 
– across 33 case-years, four historical periods, and three geopolitical blocs – indicates that the General 
Assembly forum imposes a structural demand for normative performance that operates independently of 
the speaker’s strategic intent. Every coded speech in the sample, including those delivered by 
governments under sustained international criticism, contains sufficient justificatory or normative content 
to be classified as communicative or normative action. H2, as specified, predicted normative-legal 
dominance; the data show communicative-justificatory framing in the leading register instead. The 
 

--- PAGE 59 ---
59 
structural claim holds – strategic action is absent as a dominant mode – but the characteristic form 
legitimation takes in the corpus is argumentative rather than norm-invoking. 
Of the strategies identified, counter-terrorism and securitization framing (34.3% of coded 
segments) and procedural-legal claims (27.5%) together account for nearly two-thirds of the sample. 
Explicit self-defense invocations under Article 51 of the UN Charter appear in only one coded segment; 
the characteristic pattern is the construction of necessity arguments through securitization language, with 
formal legal citation as supporting rather than primary vehicle. The temporal distribution of these 
strategies is not stable. A statistically significant shift – χ²(6) = 21.06, p = .0018 – marks the transition 
from Cold War normative dominance, when Charter invocations and sovereignty appeals accounted for 
63.6% of classified segments, to post-9/11 communicative dominance, when justificatory framing reached 
71.0% of classified content in the 2002–2013 sample. September 2001 functions in this data as a threshold 
crossing rather than a gradual drift: the counter-terrorism infrastructure that followed created a 
legitimation resource adopted rapidly across the full spectrum of the UN membership. The Post-2014 
period partially reverses this trajectory – normative-legal framing recovers to 25.9% – consistent with the 
intensification of sovereignty-revisionist argumentation among states operating in an environment of 
increased normative contestation. 
The geopolitical dimension of the analysis produces a more complex picture than either 
convergence or divergence accounts alone can accommodate. At the level of counter-terrorism rhetoric, 
convergence is unambiguous: post-2001 adoption of securitization framing is cross-bloc and not 
significantly differentiated across the three analytical groupings. At the level of legitimation register, 
however, divergence is equally robust: Western states rely predominantly on communicative justification, 
while Soviet/Eastern and Non-Aligned states rely more consistently on normative-legal invocations – 
Charter provisions, treaty authority, the non-interference principle. The Fisher’s exact pairwise

These findings make three contributions to the IR literature. At the empirical level, the analysis

UNGA legitimation discourse and more frequent than normative-legal invocation in every post-Cold War

beyond the two episodes – Kosovo and Iraq – on which it was originally grounded, demonstrating that the 
forum-level constraint it describes holds across three blocs and seven decades. At the interpretive level, 
the register differentiation finding – Western states employing quasi-legal justificatory framing while 
non-Western states invoke formal Charter-grounded norms – complicates received narratives of Western 
states as primary custodians of the ‘rules-based order’, pointing instead toward structurally conditioned 
asymmetries – of the kind Koskenniemi (2005) describes in tracing the oscillation between descending 
and ascending legal argument – that are observable in the discourse independently of any actor’s stated 
normative commitments. 
The broader significance of these findings lies in what they reveal about the durability of the 
UNGA as a legitimation arena. The General Debate remains the single venue at which every UN member 
state is obligated to appear and address a universal audience – without veto and without the procedural 
constraints of the Security Council. As the normative architecture of the postwar order has come under 
sustained pressure since 2014, the question of how states legitimize legally contested conduct before that 
audience becomes analytically important precisely because the forum continues to function. The evidence 
presented here suggests that the communicative demand of the General Assembly has not collapsed under 
normative fragmentation: even in the most recent period of the corpus, states continue to perform 
legitimation rather than abandon it. Tracking the strategies through which that performance is constructed, 
and the conditions under which the repertoire shifts, is a precondition for any systematic account of how 
international norms are contested, sustained, and reconstructed under pressure. For the period 1946–2023, 
across four historical periods and three geopolitical blocs, this analysis has provided that account – 
documenting not the collapse of legitimation under normative fragmentation, but its persistence and 
structural transformation.
